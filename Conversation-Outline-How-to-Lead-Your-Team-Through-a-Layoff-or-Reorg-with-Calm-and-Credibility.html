Navigating a Reorganization and Downsizing

Reorgs are one of the hardest things to lead through — not because the communication is complicated, but because you're being asked to hold steady for your team while you're dealing with your own uncertainty at the same time. This guide gives you the frameworks, scripts, and boundaries you need to lead with clarity and compassion, even when you don't have all the answers.

Leadership Reset: A Mindfulness Minute Before Difficult Conversations

Use this quick nervous-system regulation exercise before team meetings, 1:1s, or any difficult conversation during times of organizational change.

1. Ground Your Body

Place both feet flat on the floor. Notice the support underneath you — the chair, the ground. Let your body feel supported without doing anything.

Why it helps: Grounding your body helps your brain shift out of fight-or-flight.

2. Regulate Your Breath

Inhale for a count of 4. Hold for 2. Exhale for 6. Repeat 2–3 times.

Why it helps: Longer exhales signal safety to your nervous system and reduce reactivity.

3. Identify One Thing You Can Control

Quietly name one thing you can control today — your tone, your pace, your boundaries, or how clearly you communicate.

Why it helps: Focusing on a single point of control cuts through overwhelm and anchors your leadership presence.

4. Choose an Intention

Pick one statement to guide how you want to lead today:

  • I lead with steadiness.
  • I communicate clearly.
  • I don't take on what isn't mine.
  • I stay grounded even when emotions rise.

Why it helps: Intentions give your brain a simple, repeatable leadership anchor during uncertainty.

5. Close the Reset

Take one final slow breath. Return to the moment. Carry your intention into your next interaction.

When to Use This Reset

  • Before delivering difficult news
  • Before responding to emotional team members
  • Before entering meetings filled with unknowns
  • Anytime you feel anxious, overwhelmed, or reactive

Understanding the Impact of Reorgs and Downsizing

Reorganizations and downsizing create high uncertainty and high emotion — fear, anger, guilt, anxiety, and sometimes even relief. Leaders are caught in the middle: asked to communicate decisions they didn't make, while managing their own feelings at the same time.

You can be loyal to the business and compassionate toward people and honest about your own limits. It's not about whether the reorg is "good" or "bad." It's about how you take care of yourself and your people inside something you can't fully control.

What's In Your Control — and What Isn't

Not in Your Control In Your Control or Influence
Final headcount decisions How clearly you communicate what you do know
Exact org charts How you set expectations and boundaries
Executive messaging strategy How you model emotional regulation
How you support your team's nervous systems (pace, predictability, presence)

Reflect before you communicate:

  • What are the things about this reorg that you cannot control?
  • What are the things you can influence as a leader this week?

The Human Side: Understanding Your Team's Emotional Landscape

Common Team Reactions

  • Survivor's guilt: "Why am I still here when my colleague isn't?"
  • Hypervigilance: Scanning for "signs" they're next.
  • Withdrawal: Quiet quitting, disengagement, distraction.
  • Anger/blame: At leadership, at "the company," sometimes at you.

Your team's behavior in a reorg is often about fear, not attitude or unprofessionalism. If you treat fear like defiance, you'll respond the wrong way.

What Your Job Actually Is

Reactions are normal responses to loss and uncertainty — they are not a sign of your failure as a leader. Your job is not to "fix" their feelings. Your job is to:

  • Hold space
  • Give clarity where you can
  • Not add harm — avoid defensiveness, dismissiveness, or false promises

The 4 C's Framework for Reorg Communication

  1. Clarity — Say what you know, and what you don't know.
  2. Consistency — Repeat key messages; don't improvise wildly.
  3. Compassion — Validate impact without trashing the company.
  4. Containment — Don't overshare your own fear. Show the edges of your feelings so your team feels connected to you — but do not flood them. Use discernment about what is worth sharing and what isn't.

Showing Emotion Without Flooding Your Team

Good containment sounds like:

  • "This has been a tough week for all of us, and I'm feeling it too. That said, I'm clear on my role here, and I'm focused on supporting you through the changes."
  • "I care about this team deeply, and yes, this has been hard. I'm staying grounded and I'm here with you in this."
  • "I'm disappointed about some of the changes too, but I'm not overwhelmed. I want you to know I've got myself so I can show up for you."

What not to say:

  • "I barely slept last night. I'm terrified too."
  • "I don't know what leadership is thinking — I'm so angry."

Using Discernment About What to Share

Healthy, boundaried honesty:

  • "There are pieces of this I don't love, and there are parts I do understand from a business standpoint. I'm not here to debate the decision, but I will support you through it."
  • "Some of this information is still in motion, so I'm not going to speculate. I'll stick to what's confirmed."
  • "I'm aware of some upcoming changes, but they're not finalized. Sharing half-formed information would create unnecessary anxiety, so I'll wait until we have clarity."

Over-disclosure that erodes trust:

  • "I heard they might cut even more but no one's told me officially."
  • "I'm worried about my own job too — I just don't know what's going on."

Scripts and Conversation Guides

What to Say When You First Talk to Your Team

Goals: Acknowledge reality. Share what you can. Set expectations for what's next.

Sample script:

"I know everyone's feeling a lot right now. The organization has made changes that affect roles and teams.

Here's what I know so far:

  • [Insert confirmed facts]
  • [What is changing]

Here's what I don't know yet:

  • [List what you don't know honestly]

My commitment to you is to share updates as I get them, to be honest about what I can and can't answer, and to support you through the transition the best I can. I also want to hear your questions and concerns, even if I can't fix everything."

Answering Hard Questions You Don't Have Answers To

Don't lie. Don't over-reassure.

Phrases to use:

  • "I don't know the answer to that yet, and I don't want to make something up. Here's what I can tell you…"
  • "That's a fair question. Here's what I've been told, and here's what's still being decided."
  • "I can't promise there won't be more changes. What I can promise is that I'll share information as soon as I'm able to."

What to avoid:

  • "Everything will be fine."
  • "Nobody else is leaving." (Unless 100% confirmed and approved to say.)
  • Venting about senior leaders to try to build trust.

1:1 Conversations With Worried Team Members

A simple five-step structure for 1:1s during a reorg:

  1. Open the door — "How are you really doing with all of this?"
  2. Listen without defending — "That makes sense." / "I can see why you'd feel that way."
  3. Reflect and normalize — "A lot of people are feeling that mix of anxiety and frustration." / "So what I hear is that you're most concerned about…"
  4. Offer what you can do — "Here's what I can do for you in the next 2–3 weeks…"
  5. Close with clarity — "Our next known milestone is [insert date or details]. I'll keep you posted before then if anything shifts. I can't remove all of the uncertainty, and I wish I could. What I can do is make sure you aren't surprised by information I already know, and help you think through your options."

Leading "Survivors" After Downsizing

The team members who remain after a downsizing need just as much leadership attention as the people who left. They may be feeling guilty for staying, quietly job-hunting, or overwhelmed with extra work — while you're under pressure to "do more with less" and keep people engaged.

What to Do

  • Name it explicitly: "I know some of you might be feeling guilty that you're still here while others are not. That's a very human reaction."
  • Don't pretend nothing happened. Avoid jumping to "back to business as usual."
  • Protect them from unsustainable expectations where possible. Push back on unrealistic timelines or priorities and advocate for a realistic long-term solution to increased workload.

Phrases for Managing Workload

  • "Given the reduced headcount, here's what we'll stop or pause so we're not burning out."
  • "If we're keeping this project, we need to talk about what comes off the plate."

Boundaries for You as a Leader

Your team does not need you to be a hero. They need you to be steady and honest.

You Are Allowed To

  • Have your own feelings
  • Not be available 24/7
  • Not answer questions you aren't authorized to answer
  • Decide to leave — you are not beholden to an unhealthy work life or a job you no longer like just because you lead a team

Helpful Boundary Phrases

  • "I want to talk about this, and I also need to end at [insert time]."
  • "I can't share details about other people's situations, but I can talk with you about your role and your options."
  • "I'm not the decision-maker on that, but I can pass your feedback up and let you know what I hear back."
