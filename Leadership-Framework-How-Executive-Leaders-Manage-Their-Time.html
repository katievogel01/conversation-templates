How Executive Leaders Manage Their Time

Most high-achieving women I work with don't struggle with time because they're disorganized — they struggle because they're overloaded and running on autopilot. The shift I want to make with you isn't about squeezing more into your day. It's about moving from reactive time management to intentional time design — and that starts with how you relate to time, not how you schedule it.

The Mindset Shift: Employee vs. CEO

Employee Mindset CEO Mindset
Time is something I have to manage. Time is something I design.
My calendar dictates my day. I set the priorities.
I react to meetings, emails, and deadlines. I decide what gets my energy.
I keep up. I create conditions for high-impact work.

This isn't about title or position. It's about how you lead yourself.

Framework 1: The Time Posture Exercise

Before changing anything on your calendar, get honest about where your time is actually going — and how it feels in your body. Leaders who operate from a grounded, expansive posture make more strategic, long-term decisions instead of reactive, fear-based ones.

Step 1 — Map Your Time (5 minutes)

Draw a simple pie chart of how you typically spend your workday or workweek. Estimate the percentage of time going to each category:

  • Meetings
  • Deep, strategic work
  • Email, Slack, or reacting to others
  • Intentionally unstructured time (thinking, planning, resetting)

Look at what you drew. What stands out? What do you notice — not just intellectually, but in your body?

Step 2 — Tune Into Your Default Time Posture

Right now, if your body reflected how you currently relate to time, what would it look like?

  • Are you leaning forward, chasing the next task?
  • Are your shoulders tight, weighed down by pressure?
  • Do you feel frozen, unsure where to start?

Just notice. No fixing — just awareness. Then shake it out and let it go.

Step 3 — Shift Into Leadership Posture (30 seconds)

Sit tall. Plant your feet. Relax your shoulders and let your breath slow down. Breathe into the version of you who designs her time instead of reacting to it. Feel the difference.

Reflection Prompts

  • What changed? What feels different in your body?
  • What's the gap between what you saw on paper and what you felt physically?
  • What does this posture tell you about how you want to relate to time going forward?

Framework 2: Identifying Your Time Traps

With honest awareness as your foundation, identify the single biggest thing stealing your time right now. Not five things — one thing.

Then go one level deeper:

  • What is keeping me in this pattern?
  • What part of me is allowing this?

Naming the trap is the first step to dismantling it. Common time traps include over-meeting, perfectionism, saying yes by default, constant context-switching, and never having protected thinking time. Which one feels most familiar?

Framework 3: The 3 Principles of Executive Time Design

Principle 1 — Energy First, Time Second

The biggest mistake high-achievers make is treating time as their most limited resource. It's not — energy is. You can have hours in your day, but if your energy is drained, those hours mean nothing.

Key shift: Stop asking "Do I have time for this?" and start asking "Do I have the energy for this?"

Implementation: Schedule your highest-stakes, most cognitively demanding work during peak energy hours. Know your rhythms — if you do your best thinking in the morning, protect that time fiercely. Reserve lower-energy windows for meetings, admin, and routine tasks.

Principle 2 — Decide Once, Implement 100 Times

Most time stress comes from making the same decision over and over. High-level leaders reduce decision fatigue by creating default rules that remove the need to constantly re-decide.

Implementation — examples of default rules:

  • Meeting-free mornings (set it once, hold it consistently)
  • No checking email before 10 AM
  • 1:1 calls only on Tuesdays
  • No desktop notifications during focus blocks
  • Phone put away — not silenced, put away — during deep work

Ask yourself: Where do you waste time re-deciding the same thing over and over? What is one rule you can create today that removes that recurring decision entirely?

Principle 3 — Design for Impact, Not Just Productivity

Being productive and being impactful are not the same thing. Productivity is getting things done. Impact is moving the needle on what actually matters.

Key shift: Stop measuring your day by output volume. Start measuring by whether you moved something meaningful forward.

Prioritization Tool: The Eisenhower Matrix

Urgent Not Urgent
Important Do it now. These are crises, deadlines, and high-stakes decisions. Schedule it. This is where strategic work, development, and planning live. Protect this quadrant.
Not Important Delegate it. These feel urgent but don't require your direct attention. Eliminate it. These are distractions and time-fillers that add no real value.

Most overloaded leaders are living in the top-left quadrant — urgent and important — and never making it to the top-right, where the real strategic leadership work happens. The goal is to shift time and energy into that quadrant deliberately.

Daily Anchor: The End-of-Day Test

Before you close your laptop, ask yourself one question:

Did I do something today that my future self — or my business, my team, my leadership — will thank me for?

If the answer is yes, it was a good day regardless of how full your inbox still is. If the answer is no, tomorrow is a chance to redesign.

Weekly Reflection Prompts

  1. Where did I operate from the CEO mindset this week? Where did I slip back into the employee mindset?
  2. Which of my time traps showed up? What triggered it?
  3. Did I protect my peak energy hours for high-impact work?
  4. What default rule do I need to create or reinforce?
  5. If I had to make one shift in how I design my time next week, what would it be?
