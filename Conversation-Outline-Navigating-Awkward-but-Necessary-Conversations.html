Navigating Awkward but Necessary Conversations: The CLEAR Framework

Category: Difficult Conversations / Team Communication / Feedback & Accountability

Best used when: You need to address a team member's behavior — unprofessional conduct, interrupting others, passive-aggressive communication, personal hygiene, or boundary issues — and the stakes are high enough that avoiding it will quietly damage the relationship or the team. Use this any time you're dreading a conversation but know it has to happen.

Introduction

If you've ever fumbled a difficult conversation, it's not a personality flaw — you just didn't have the right structure. Avoiding the conversation doesn't preserve the relationship; it quietly damages it. This framework gives you a repeatable structure and real scripts so you can address issues clearly, protect the relationship, and actually change behavior.

The Framework: CLEAR

Adapted from Peter Hawkins' CLEAR coaching methodology and the SBI (Situation–Behavior–Impact) feedback model — blending coaching-based dialogue with clear, behavior-focused feedback.

C — Context

Open the conversation with honesty and respect — signal that this matters.

  • "Do you have 10 minutes for something important?"
  • "I want to talk about something a little awkward but necessary."
  • "I'm bringing this up because I respect you and want us to work well together."
  • "This isn't easy to say, but it's important we address it."

L — Lay Out Observable Facts

Name only what you saw or heard — no labels, no assumptions, no intent.

  • "What I've noticed is…"
  • "In the last two meetings…"
  • "Yesterday, when X happened…"
  • "Here's what I observed…"
  • "I want to stick to the facts of what I saw/heard…"

E — Effect (Business + Relationship Impact)

Name the real-world consequences of the behavior.

  • "The impact is…"
  • "What this creates for the team is…"
  • "Here's how it affects the work…"
  • "What ends up happening is…"
  • "This makes it harder for others to…"

A — Ask for Their Perspective

Pause and genuinely listen — this is not rhetorical.

  • "What was going on for you?"
  • "How do you see it?"
  • "What's your perspective here?"
  • "Is there something I'm missing?"
  • "Help me understand your side."

R — Request + Reset Expectations

Be clear, concrete, and forward-focused.

  • "What I need going forward is…"
  • "Here's what I'm asking you to do differently…"
  • "Moving forward, I expect…"
  • "Can you commit to…"
  • "Let's align on what this looks like next time."

Quick-Reference Scripts

Unprofessional Behavior (phone use, side conversations, tone)

"In the last two meetings, I noticed you were on your phone and had side conversations. The impact is people disengage and the meeting loses momentum. What was going on for you?" (pause) "Going forward, I need full engagement — phones away unless you tell me in advance something urgent is happening."

Interrupting / Talking Over Others

In the moment: "[Name], can you hold your thought — let [name] finish."

Private follow-up: "You jumped in while Sarah was speaking twice yesterday. The impact is she contributes less. I don't think this is your intention, but when you respond quickly, other people stop offering ideas. What's your read on that?" (pause) "What I'm asking is that you slow down a bit in meetings to let everyone finish their thoughts first and make space for everyone's ideas."

When It Gets Hard

Situation What to Say
Defensiveness "I'm not questioning your intent. I'm naming the impact."
Tears "I can see this is landing hard. Do you want a short pause, or should we continue? We do need to finish the conversation."
Anger "I want this to stay constructive. Let's slow it down."

Closing the Conversation

A strong close does two things: locks in behavior change and protects the relationship. Use this structure to end every awkward conversation.

Clear Commitment

"So we're aligned that you'll do X." Name the behavior clearly. Avoid vague agreements.

Timeline

  • "Starting immediately."
  • "By Friday."

Check-In

  • "Let's reconnect next Wednesday and see how it's going."

Support

  • "Here's what I can do to support you."
  • "Let me know what would be helpful as you work on this."

Connection — The Trust Builder

End by reconnecting at a human level. This does not undo the message — it anchors the relationship.

  • "I appreciate you being open to this conversation."
  • "I know these aren't easy topics, and I respect how you handled it."
  • "I'm glad we talked — I want us to work well together."
  • "I value working with you, which is why I wanted to address this directly."
  • "This doesn't change how I see your contributions — I just want us aligned."

If the conversation was tense or emotional:
"I know that wasn't an easy conversation. I appreciate you staying in it with me."

To reinforce psychological safety:
"If anything about this feels unclear or comes up later, let me know. I'd rather talk about it than let it sit."

Follow Up in Writing

After the conversation, send a brief email to document alignment. Include: appreciation for the conversation, the agreed-upon behavior, timeline and any check-ins, and support offered.

Example email:
"Thanks for taking the time to talk today. I appreciate your openness in the conversation. As discussed, going forward you'll do X. We'll reconnect next Wednesday to check in. Please let me know if you need anything from me as you work through this. I'm glad we were able to align."
