How to Read Body Language Cues

Most communication happens before you open your mouth — and once you start noticing that, conversations change completely. This guide pulls from Vanessa Van Edwards' research on cues and gives you the practical tools to read a room, adjust your own signals, and show up with the kind of presence that actually lands.

What Are Cues?

Cues are the silent signals we send through body language, tone, and word choice. We read them constantly — even when we don't realize it. And we're always sending them too, shaping how others perceive us whether we're aware of it or not. The goal isn't to manufacture a performance; it's to make sure what you're putting out there actually matches what you mean.

Conversation starters:

  • Have you ever misread someone's body language — or had yours misinterpreted? What happened?
  • When you think about how you show up in meetings or important conversations, do you think your body language and tone match your intentions?

The Warmth–Competence Balance

The most effective communicators strike a balance between warmth and competence. Lean too far toward warmth and you risk being seen as overly agreeable or not taken seriously. Lean too far toward competence and you can come across as intimidating or cold. The sweet spot is where people feel both respected and at ease with you.

Conversation starters:

  • Have you ever felt like you needed to prove your competence in a conversation? How did you approach it?
  • Do you tend to lean more toward warmth or competence in how you communicate? What are the pros and cons of that for you?

Practical Techniques for Stronger Communication

Body Language

Technique: Keep an open posture — no crossed arms. Face the person speaking directly and minimize fidgeting. Open body positioning signals that you're present and not on the defensive.

Conversation starter: What's something someone has done with their body language that made you feel heard — or dismissed — in a conversation?

Facial Expressions

Technique: A slight head tilt communicates genuine engagement. A natural (not forced) smile creates warmth and puts people at ease. Your face is often the first thing someone reads — make sure it's saying what you want.

Conversation starter: Have you ever caught yourself making a face you didn't intend to in a conversation?

Vocal Tone

Technique: Lowering your pitch slightly at the end of a sentence signals confidence and authority. Upward inflections can read as uncertainty, even when you're sure of yourself. Record yourself occasionally — most people are surprised by what they hear.

Conversation starter: Have you ever noticed how someone's voice affected how seriously you took their message?

Hand Gestures

Technique: Using deliberate hand gestures makes you appear more dynamic and engaged. Gestures help emphasize key points and keep your listener's attention anchored to what you're saying.

Conversation starter: Do you use your hands a lot when you talk? Have you noticed how different personalities or cultures use gestures differently?

Reading Cues in Others

Once you've got your own signals under control, you can start picking up on what's happening in the room. The three biggest nonverbal tells to watch for:

Cue What to Watch For What It Often Signals
Mirroring The other person subtly copies your posture, gestures, or pace Rapport and connection — they're in sync with you
Foot positioning Feet pointing toward or away from you or the exit Interest and engagement vs. readiness to disengage
Microexpressions Fleeting facial expressions that pass in under a second Emotions that haven't been filtered — often the truest read you'll get

Conversation starters:

  • Have you ever noticed someone mirroring your movements? Did it change how the conversation felt?
  • Do you think you're good at reading people's emotions? What do you pay attention to most?

Self-Reflection Practices

The fastest way to improve is to start paying attention to your own cues — not just in the moment, but after the fact.

Seek Feedback

Ask a trusted colleague or friend how you come across in conversations. Be specific: ask about your tone, your eye contact, whether you seem distracted. Most people never get this feedback unless they ask for it directly.

Conversation starter: Have you ever been surprised by feedback about how you communicate?

Practice In-the-Moment Awareness

In your next high-stakes conversation, check in with yourself mid-way: What is my posture doing right now? Is my tone matching my intent? Small real-time adjustments compound over time into lasting habits.

Conversation starter: When do you feel most confident in how you communicate? What do you think changes in your body language or tone in those moments?

Study Strong Communicators

Watch speakers or leaders you respect — not just for what they say, but for how they hold themselves, how they pace their words, when they pause. Deconstructing what works is one of the best ways to borrow it.

Conversation starter: Who's someone you think is a great communicator? What specifically makes them effective?

Quick-Reference: Cue Checklist

Area Strong Signal Weak Signal
Posture Open, relaxed, facing the speaker Crossed arms, turned away, hunched
Facial expression Head tilt, natural smile, engaged eyes Blank expression, furrowed brow, avoiding eye contact
Vocal tone Steady, pitch drops at sentence end Upward inflection, trailing off, rushed speech
Gestures Deliberate, in the torso zone Fidgeting, hands hidden, excessive movement
Listening cues Mirroring, leaning slightly in, nodding Feet angled away, distracted glances, no response
